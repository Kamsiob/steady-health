# CONTENT.md: the cards behind Ask a question

Final copy. Hand-written, fixed, shipped in the app. The model only picks which card to show (AI.md job 5); it never writes one. Every card explains a mechanism and prescribes nothing. Every caution lives inside the card, not behind a link. Reading level target: sixth grade. No banned words. Each card ends with a one-line source the person can look up.

## 0. How Steady Health works
Summary: The whole app in four short paragraphs.

Four abilities, three things a day. The app keeps track of four things your body does: getting up, going, carrying, and staying steady. Those sit at the top of every day. Under them are the three small things: weigh in whenever it suits you. Say how the day went, in a sentence. Move for two minutes, or longer if you want. When a thing is done it turns green and stays done. The empty days are just days.

Your weight is one of the levers, not the score. It gets smoothed, so one heavy morning doesn't move it. It matters because eighteen fewer pounds is eighteen fewer pounds to carry up the stairs, and the app says it that way.

Your walks get longer on your terms. After two walks that felt easy, the next one is offered. You can say not yet as often as you like. If you're sore the next day, the app suggests something shorter. That's how bodies build.

Everything stays on your phone. There is no account, no server, and no copy anywhere else. If you turn on the optional reader, it runs on your phone too, and it only ever sorts your words and writes your Sunday note. It never sees your weight and never gives advice.

Once a month there's a short check. Ten minutes, a chair and a wall. It tells you what you can do now and what it was before. Staying the same counts.

Source: this app's own rules, which are open source.

## 1. Why the scale jumps overnight
Summary: Four pounds overnight is water, not fat.

A salty dinner holds water. A big meal is still on its way through. A hard walk leaves your muscles holding extra fluid while they repair. Where you are in a cycle changes water too. Any of these can move the scale two, three, four pounds by morning.

Fat is different. Gaining or losing a pound of fat takes days of eating more or less than your body uses. It cannot happen overnight. So a jump in the morning number is almost never what it looks like.

That's why Steady shows the smoothed number first. It takes every morning into account and moves slowly, so the water swings mostly cancel out and what's left is the real direction.

Source: normal day-to-day body weight variation is around one to two kilograms; see the Hacker's Diet by John Walker for the smoothing method.

## 2. Why weight goes flat
Summary: A flat stretch around month six is normal and expected.

Most people's weight comes down fastest in the first few months, then slows, then goes flat for a while, often around six months in. This is not a sign that something stopped working. As weight comes down, the body uses a little less energy at rest, appetite tends to come up, and daily movement can quietly drop. The line levels out.

Changing diets does not change this. A 2024 study switched people between diets at the plateau and the flat stretch stayed flat.

What does keep moving is what you can do. Walks get longer, standing up gets quicker. Steady shows those beside the weight line for exactly this reason.

Source: Stanford crossover trial, Scientific Reports, 2024; real-world data from 667 programme users showing decrease, plateau, and partial regain as the most common pattern.

## 3. Does two minutes really count?
Summary: Short bouts add up, and people keep doing them.

Yes. A 2025 review of eleven trials found that bouts of five minutes or less, done a couple of times a day, improved fitness in adults who had been inactive. Total time was often under half an hour a week. And 83 percent of people stuck with it, which is rare for any exercise study.

A separate study of 25,000 people who did no formal exercise found that about four minutes a day of brisk everyday movement, like climbing stairs quickly or walking fast to the bus, was linked to a noticeably lower chance of dying over the study period.

The barrier was never the minutes. It was believing they had to be more.

Source: Rodriguez et al., British Journal of Sports Medicine, 2025; Stamatakis et al., Nature Medicine, 2022.

## 4. How intermittent fasting works
Summary: An eating window, not a magic hour.

Intermittent fasting means eating only during certain hours, often eight hours a day, or eating very little on some days. Studies that compare it with simply eating less across the whole day find about the same weight change. Most of its effect comes from eating less overall, not from the timing. What matters most is whether you can keep it up.

It is not for everyone. Skip it if you are pregnant or breastfeeding, if you take insulin or certain diabetes tablets, if you have ever had an eating disorder, or if you are under eighteen. If you take any regular medication, ask whoever prescribes it before changing when you eat.

Steady Health does not track eating windows and will not suggest one.

Source: reviews comparing time-restricted eating with continuous calorie reduction; see the 2022 New England Journal of Medicine trial by Liu et al.

## 5. Muscle and weight-loss medications
Summary: When weight comes off fast, some of it is muscle. Two things protect it.

On medications like semaglutide and tirzepatide, a meaningful share of the weight lost can be muscle rather than fat. In one large trial it was close to half; in another about a quarter. Losing muscle makes it harder to stand up, carry things, and keep weight off later.

Two habits protect most of it. Something with protein at most meals, and some strength work, like standing up from a chair or wall push-ups, a couple of times a week. That's the whole reason the strength sets in this app exist.

Steady Health never tracks the medication itself. Your prescriber's tools do that better.

Source: Neeland et al., Diabetes, Obesity and Metabolism, 2024, reporting lean mass changes in the STEP-1 and SURMOUNT-1 trials.

## 6. Why the app shows a smoothed number
Summary: So one heavy morning doesn't feel like a verdict.

Smoothing takes every morning's weight into account and gives the newest one only a small say. The result moves slowly and follows the real direction. Day-to-day water swings mostly cancel out.

Research on people who weigh themselves daily finds it helps, but only when the daily swings don't discourage them. The smoothed number is what makes daily weighing bearable.

If you would rather see no numbers at all, there is a switch in settings. The app then uses words like "a little lower" instead.

Source: exponentially weighted moving average as described in the Hacker's Diet; self-weighing evidence from a 10,000-user cohort in JMIR, 2021.

## 7. Why there are no calories here
Summary: Counting works for some people and hurts others. Steady leaves it out.

Calorie apps turn every meal into a number to beat, and a study of nearly 60,000 posts about the biggest ones found the same feelings over and over: shame, blame, and giving up. Among people with eating disorders, most said the most popular calorie app made things worse.

Steady Health tracks what your body can do, your walks, your weight, and how your days go. That's enough to see what's changing. If a food pattern matters, it shows up in your own words, in the Sunday note, without a number attached.

If food is a struggle right now, the resources page in settings has a helpline. No questions asked.

Source: Bondaronek et al., British Journal of Health Psychology, 2025; Levinson et al., Eating Behaviors, 2017.

## 8. What the talk test is
Summary: If you can talk but not sing, you're at a good pace.

After a walk, Steady asks whether you could have held a conversation. That's the talk test, and it's a real measure, not a guess. If you could talk easily, the pace was gentle. If you could talk but had to catch your breath, it was moderate, which is where most of the benefit is. If you couldn't talk, it was hard.

For most people, most of the time, the moderate pace is plenty. The app uses your answers to decide when to offer a longer walk, and it never pushes past "just about."

Source: the talk test as a marker of the first ventilatory threshold, validated in work supported by the American Council on Exercise.

## 9. Why walks get longer slowly
Summary: Big jumps cause injuries. Small steps don't.

The old advice was to add ten percent a week. A study of over five thousand runners found that what actually predicts injury is a single session that's much longer than anything you've done in the last month. A big jump, not a steady climb.

So the app never offers a walk more than about ten percent longer than your longest one in the past thirty days, and only after two walks that felt easy. Repeating a walk is progress. Saying not yet is fine.

If you're sore the next day, the app suggests a shorter one. Bodies build in the rest between, not during.

Source: Frandsen et al., British Journal of Sports Medicine, 2025.

## 10. What to do after a break
Summary: Ease back in. Nothing is lost.

A week off changes nothing; often it helps. After two or three weeks, breathing fitness has slipped a little but strength is mostly still there. After a month or more, both have slipped, and the tendons and joints take longer to catch back up than the muscles do, which is why the first couple of weeks back are when people get hurt.

So after a break, the app suggests a slightly shorter walk than where you left off, for a couple of weeks, then opens up again. Your weight line and your history are exactly where you left them.

Source: detraining reviews on aerobic and strength loss over one week to several months.

## 11. Why staying the same counts
Summary: Holding a number for a year is not nothing.

Most apps only celebrate going up. But muscle strength drops by somewhere between one and a half and five percent a year from midlife onward if nothing is done about it. So a chair-stand count that is the same in December as it was in March is not a flat line. It is a year of that drop not happening.

That is why this app reports Same the same way it reports Better. If you are holding a number, you are doing the work.

Source: reviews of age-related muscle and strength loss from midlife.

## 12. Why the app asks what you want to be able to do
Summary: Because a number only means something next to a life.

Physical therapists use a simple tool: the person names three to five things they are struggling with, and rates each out of ten. It is one of the better ways to measure whether treatment worked, because the person picks what counts.

This app does the same. You said what you wanted; the app tracks those things and asks again once a month. Seven seconds off a chair-stand time is abstract. Getting off the floor without your hands is not.

Source: the Patient-Specific Functional Scale, used widely in physical therapy.
